New Zealand Weather Warnings Explained

The difference between a Watch, an Orange Warning and a Red Warning — and why Red is issued so rarely.

New Zealand's weather warning system is run by MetService and built as a ladder of increasing certainty and severity. Knowing which rung you are on tells you what you are supposed to do.

The Ladder

  • Severe Weather Outlook — issued several days ahead, flagging the potential for severe weather. Low certainty; useful for planning, not for action.
  • Severe Weather Watch — severe weather is possible. Conditions are being monitored and could develop into warning-level weather. Pay attention and start preparing.
  • Severe Weather Warning (Orange) — severe weather is expected or occurring. Take action: secure property, avoid unnecessary travel, follow local advice.
  • Red Warning — the most extreme category, used rarely. Significant impact and danger to life are expected. Act immediately and follow Civil Defence instructions.

Why Red Warnings Are Rare

The Red Warning tier exists precisely because it is uncommon. A warning level that fires frequently loses its power to change behaviour — the well-documented "warning fatigue" problem. By reserving Red for genuinely exceptional events, MetService keeps it meaningful: if a Red Warning is issued for your area, the expectation is that you stop what you are doing and act.

Cyclone Gabrielle in February 2023 is the clearest recent example, and the scale of what followed — 11 deaths and only the third national state of emergency in New Zealand's history — illustrates the kind of event the tier is for.

Warnings Versus Emergency Instructions

An important distinction that catches people out: MetService issues weather warnings; Civil Defence issues emergency instructions.

MetService tells you severe weather is coming and how severe. Evacuation orders, road closures, and states of emergency come from Civil Defence Emergency Management groups and local councils. During a major event you need both channels — the meteorological picture and the local instruction — and the second one is what tells you whether to leave your house.

New Zealand's Particular Hazards

New Zealand's warning categories cover rain, wind, snow, and thunderstorms, and the country's geography makes some of these unusually consequential:

  • Heavy rain in steep hill country produces rapid flooding and landslides, as in Cyclone Bola and Gabrielle.
  • Severe gales are amplified by the country's position in the Roaring Forties and funnelled through Cook Strait, as in the Wahine storm.
  • Heavy snow can reach unusually low elevations, as in the Great Snow of 2011.
  • Ex-tropical cyclones arriving from the north are a recurring seasonal risk, particularly in La Niña years.

How to Actually Use This

The practical habit: check Outlooks for planning, treat a Watch as your cue to prepare, treat an Orange Warning as your cue to act, and treat a Red Warning as your cue to follow instructions immediately rather than form your own assessment. And always pair MetService's picture with what your local Civil Defence group is saying.

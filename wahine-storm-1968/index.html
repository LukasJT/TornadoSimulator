The Wahine Storm and Disaster of 1968

Extreme storm · Wellington, New Zealand · April 10, 1968 · 51 killed

On April 10, 1968, an extraordinarily violent storm — formed by the merger of the remnants of ex-tropical Cyclone Giselle with an intense southerly system — battered Wellington, New Zealand, with some of the strongest winds ever recorded in the country. At the height of the storm, the inter-island ferry TEV Wahine was driven onto rocks and later capsized at the entrance to Wellington Harbour, killing 51 people in New Zealand's worst peacetime maritime disaster.

What Happened

The storm formed from an unusual and extremely dangerous combination: the remnants of a tropical cyclone moving down from the north merged with a powerful southerly storm system, dramatically amplifying the winds over central New Zealand. An automatic weather station on the hills above Wellington recorded a gust of roughly 275 km/h — among the highest wind speeds ever measured in New Zealand.

The Wahine, one of the main ferries linking New Zealand's North and South Islands, was approaching Wellington Harbour as the storm reached its peak. In the extreme winds and heavy seas, the ship lost power and steering and was driven onto Barrett Reef at the harbour entrance.

The Sinking

After striking the reef, the crippled Wahine drifted for several hours as the storm continued to batter Wellington. In the early afternoon, with thousands of people watching helplessly from the shore, the ship began listing badly and eventually capsized in the harbour. Many passengers were forced into the water in extreme conditions, and some lifeboats capsized or were swamped in the rough seas near the shore.

  • 51 fatalities, most drowned in the harbour or surf near the wreck
  • Extreme winds of roughly 275 km/h recorded in the Wellington hills
  • Widespread additional storm damage across the greater Wellington region
  • New Zealand's worst peacetime maritime disaster

Why It Was So Deadly

Several factors compounded the tragedy: the extreme, near-record winds; the ship's loss of power at the worst possible moment near a harbour entrance studded with rocks; the difficulty of a safe evacuation in such violent conditions; and the fact that many people ended up in cold, storm-tossed water close to shore but still in extremely dangerous surf. The disaster unfolded in view of a shocked city, which sharpened its impact on New Zealand's national consciousness.

A Storm Still Studied Today

Meteorologists still study the Wahine storm as one of the most intense wind events ever documented over New Zealand, and it remains the reference point whenever an unusually powerful storm approaches Wellington, a city already known for being one of the windiest capital cities in the world. The disaster also led to significant reviews of maritime safety procedures for New Zealand's inter-island ferry services.

Aftermath

The Wahine disaster remains deeply embedded in New Zealand's cultural memory, commemorated annually in Wellington, and is frequently referenced in discussions of the country's most extreme storms alongside more recent events like Cyclone Bola and Cyclone Gabrielle. It stands as a stark illustration of how a single extreme storm, striking at the wrong moment, can produce a disaster far beyond what wind speed alone would suggest.

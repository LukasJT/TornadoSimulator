How UK Storms Get Their Names

Why Storm Eunice had a name, why Q, U, X, Y and Z never appear, and what naming is actually for.

Britain named its first storm — Abigail — in November 2015. Before that, UK storms were described rather than named, and usually only in retrospect: the Great Storm of 1987, the Burns' Day Storm, the Braer Storm. The switch to advance naming was a deliberate communications decision, and it worked.

Why Name Storms at All

Naming is not meteorology — a named storm is not inherently more dangerous than an unnamed one. It is risk communication. The reasoning:

  • A single agreed name means every broadcaster, newspaper, and social media post refers to the same system, rather than "the storm," "the low," or "tonight's weather."
  • Named storms are demonstrably more memorable and more shareable, so warnings travel further and faster.
  • A name lets warnings be issued and tracked over several days without ambiguity about which system is meant.
  • Research following the system's introduction found meaningfully higher public awareness of warnings for named storms.

The problem being solved was specific: the UK had a long history of severe windstorms where the meteorology was understood in advance but the public did not act. 1987 and 1990 both produced considerable criticism of how warnings had been communicated.

How the System Works

The naming group is run jointly by three national weather services: the UK's Met Office, Ireland's Met Éireann, and the Netherlands' KNMI, which joined later. Because these countries share the same Atlantic storm track, joint naming avoids the confusion of one system carrying three different names on three sides of the North Sea.

Each year a list is published before the season begins, running alphabetically and alternating between male and female names, skipping Q, U, X, Y and Z in line with international convention. When forecasters expect a system to warrant an amber or red warning — meaning a genuine risk to life or serious disruption — it takes the next unused name on the list.

Where the Names Come From

Many of the names on each list are drawn from public suggestions submitted to the participating weather services, which is partly a public-engagement exercise in its own right: people who suggested a name tend to pay attention when it appears in a forecast.

"Named" Does Not Mean "Worst"

A common misreading is that a named storm is automatically more severe than an unnamed one. Naming is triggered by expected impact warnings, not by any absolute intensity threshold — so a moderate storm arriving over a densely populated area at rush hour may be named while a meteorologically more intense system tracking over open sea is not.

It also means historical comparison is awkward: Storm Eunice in 2022 set England's highest recorded gust and had a name; the 1987 and 1703 storms were both far deadlier and had none, because no such system existed.

Reading the Warning Colours

Naming sits alongside the Met Office's yellow, amber and red warning tiers. Yellow means be aware, amber means be prepared for disruption and possible risk to life, and red — used rarely — means take action, dangerous weather is expected. Eunice in 2022 carried the first red warning ever issued for London.

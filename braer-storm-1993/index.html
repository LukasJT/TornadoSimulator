The Braer Storm of 1993

In January 1993, a storm system in the North Atlantic near Scotland deepened to a central pressure of approximately 914 hPa — the lowest pressure ever recorded for an extratropical cyclone in the North Atlantic basin, and a figure comparable to an intense tropical hurricane. It is known as the Braer Storm, after an oil tanker that had run aground off Shetland days before it arrived.

A Record That Still Stands

Atmospheric pressure at the centre of a storm is the standard shorthand for its intensity — the lower the pressure, the more violent the pressure gradient and therefore the winds around it. Most vigorous North Atlantic winter storms bottom out somewhere in the 950s or 940s hPa. The Braer Storm's roughly 914 hPa put it in genuinely exceptional territory, and it remains the benchmark for the deepest extratropical low measured in the region.

The Braer Connection

On January 5, 1993, the oil tanker MV Braer lost power in heavy seas and ran aground on the southern tip of Shetland, spilling its cargo of crude oil. A major environmental response was underway when, days later, the record-breaking storm arrived over the same waters, bringing sustained hurricane-force winds and enormous seas to Shetland.

  • Central pressure of approximately 914 hPa, a North Atlantic record
  • Sustained hurricane-force winds across Shetland and surrounding waters
  • Extreme wave heights recorded across the northern North Sea
  • Salt spray carried far inland, damaging vegetation across Shetland

The Oil Spill Twist

The storm produced a genuinely counterintuitive environmental outcome. Rather than spreading the Braer's spilled oil into a longer-lasting catastrophe, the sheer violence of the sea state is generally credited with dispersing and breaking down the oil far faster than would otherwise have happened. Combined with the relatively light grade of crude involved, the long-term environmental damage from what could have been a major spill was significantly less than initially feared — one of the few cases where a record-setting storm is thought to have reduced a disaster rather than compounding it.

Why Shetland Fared Relatively Well

Despite hosting a record-breaking storm, Shetland's casualty toll was low. The islands are among the windiest inhabited places in the UK, and local building practice, infrastructure, and public expectations are all adapted to severe wind in a way that lower-latitude parts of Britain are not. A storm of that intensity striking southern England would have produced a dramatically different outcome — much the same lesson visible in the contrast between the 1987 Great Storm and routine Shetland winter weather.

Ireland sits at the receiving end of the North Atlantic storm track, which makes windstorms — and decaying hurricanes carried northeast by the jet stream — by far its dominant severe weather hazard. Flooding, both coastal and river, is the second. Snow and extreme cold are rare but disproportionately disruptive when they arrive. This index tracks Ireland's most significant events, with full write-ups for the biggest.

Record-Breaking Events

The Night of the Big Wind

· January 6-7, 1839

The most severe storm in Ireland's recorded history, killing hundreds and damaging a large share of Dublin's housing — and later accepted as legal proof of age for the 1909 pension. Read the full story →

Hurricane Debbie

· September 16, 1961

An ex-hurricane brought gusts around 181 km/h to Malin Head and killed more than a dozen people, in Ireland's deadliest storm of the modern era. Read the full story →

Storm Ophelia

· October 16, 2017

Another ex-hurricane, killing three in Ireland and prompting an unprecedented nationwide red warning that closed schools and transport in advance. Read the full story →

The Beast from the East and Storm Emma

· February-March 2018

An extreme cold outbreak collided with an Atlantic storm, producing blizzards and Ireland's first nationwide red snow warning. Read the full story →

Other Notable Events

  • Hurricane Charley (August 1986) — ex-hurricane remnants brought catastrophic rainfall and some of the worst flooding on record to Dublin and the east.
  • Winter floods (November-December 2015) — a prolonged sequence of Atlantic storms produced severe and sustained flooding along the Shannon and across the midlands.
  • The big freeze (December 2010) — exceptional cold brought prolonged sub-zero temperatures, burst pipes and widespread water shortages.
  • Storm Darwin (February 2014) — severe winds caused extensive forestry losses and left hundreds of thousands without power.
  • Storm Éowyn and successors — recent Atlantic windstorms have repeatedly tested the electricity network, particularly in the west.

Key Facts

  • Ireland participates in the joint storm-naming group with the UK Met Office and the Netherlands' KNMI, which is why Irish and British storms share names.
  • Ex-hurricanes are a recurring Irish hazard: the country sits where the jet stream frequently captures decaying tropical systems and carries them northeast, as with Debbie in 1961 and Ophelia in 2017.
  • Met Éireann issues yellow, orange and red warnings; red is reserved for conditions posing a danger to life and is issued rarely.
  • Ireland records occasional tornadoes, almost all weak, for broadly the same reasons as the UK — frequent fronts and convergence lines, but insufficient instability for violent storms.

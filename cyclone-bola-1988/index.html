Cyclone Bola — New Zealand, 1988

In March 1988, ex-tropical Cyclone Bola stalled over New Zealand's North Island for days, dumping extraordinary amounts of rain on the East Cape and Gisborne region and triggering catastrophic flooding and landslides. It became one of the costliest weather disasters in New Zealand history at the time and remains a benchmark event for East Coast flooding decades later.

What Happened

Bola formed as a tropical cyclone in the Coral Sea before tracking south and weakening into an ex-tropical low as it approached New Zealand. Rather than moving through quickly, the system stalled over the North Island for an unusually long period, continuously drawing in moisture and dumping torrential rain on the same areas for days on end. Some of the worst-affected areas recorded around 900mm of rain in a matter of days — an extreme total even by the standards of New Zealand's wettest regions.

Damage and Impact

The East Cape and Gisborne region, with its steep, erosion-prone hill country, was overwhelmed by the rainfall. Thousands of landslides stripped hillsides bare, burying roads, destroying farmland, and sending huge volumes of silt and debris into rivers and eventually the sea. Forestry plantations across the region were devastated, and several people were killed.

  • Several fatalities across the affected region
  • Extreme rainfall totals around 900mm in the worst-hit areas
  • Widespread, severe landslides across the East Cape hill country
  • Major, long-lasting damage to farmland and forestry

A Benchmark for Later Storms

For more than three decades, Cyclone Bola remained the reference point New Zealand meteorologists and emergency managers used when discussing worst-case flooding scenarios for the East Coast of the North Island. When Cyclone Gabrielle struck a similar part of the country in February 2023, comparisons to Bola were immediate and constant, with many describing Gabrielle's flooding and landslide damage as comparable to, or in some areas worse than, the 1988 disaster.

Why Stalled Systems Are So Dangerous

Bola illustrates a hazard pattern that shows up repeatedly in the world's worst flood disasters: a storm's peak wind speed matters far less than how long it sits in one place. A weaker, slow-moving system that stalls over the same catchment for days can produce far more total rainfall — and far more catastrophic flooding — than a much more intense storm that moves through quickly.

Aftermath

Recovery across the East Cape and Gisborne region took years, particularly for the forestry and farming sectors, and the disaster prompted significant changes to New Zealand's approach to land-use planning and erosion control in steep, flood-prone hill country. Cyclone Bola remains one of the defining natural disasters in the region's modern history.

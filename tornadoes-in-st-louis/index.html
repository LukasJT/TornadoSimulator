Tornadoes in St. Louis

A chronological guide to tornadoes in St. Louis, including landmark events, local patterns and official research sources.

Scope: This article covers St. Louis and its surrounding metropolitan area. Tornado tracks often cross city, county, state or provincial boundaries.

A city and metro tornado chronology

St. Louis has experienced tornadoes through several eras of forecasting, construction and urban growth. The entries below are landmarks rather than a complete list. They show how the regional threat has appeared in the historical record and why a metro-wide view is more useful than a city-limits-only count.

1896

St. Louis–East St. Louis F4

The May 27 tornado crossed the urban riverfront and killed 255 people, one of the deadliest tornadoes in U.S. history.

1927

St. Louis F4

A September tornado cut across the city and killed 79 people.

2011

Good Friday EF4

A tornado crossed the northern metro and struck Lambert–St. Louis International Airport.

The pattern behind the dates

St. Louis sits in the Mississippi River corridor where rolling terrain meets broad floodplains. Here, moist Gulf air and strong central U.S. storm systems can support long-track supercells. The main window is March through June, with an active secondary fall season, but the event list shows why residents should follow current forecasts instead of treating tornado season as a fixed calendar.

Urban growth changes exposure. A path that once crossed fields may now cross schools, warehouses, apartment buildings and major roads. Population growth does not necessarily mean tornadoes are becoming more frequent at one address; it means a similar path can affect more people and infrastructure.

How to investigate another St. Louis tornado

Start with the date and location in an official storm-event archive. Then check the local forecast office’s damage survey for the path, rating and evidence. Contemporary reporting can explain the community impact, but the survey is the better source for meteorological details.

Historical totals should be read carefully. Reporting practices, radar coverage and damage-survey methods have changed. The Fujita scale used before 2007 and the Enhanced Fujita scale used afterward are related damage scales, but their labels should not be treated as instrument readings.

The United Kingdom is not usually associated with extreme weather, but it experiences a genuine mix of hazards: windstorms off the Atlantic capable of killing dozens and felling millions of trees, flash floods that can destroy a village in an hour, prolonged winter flooding that can leave farmland underwater for weeks, and — perhaps surprisingly — more tornadoes per unit area than almost any other country on Earth, even though the overwhelming majority are weak. This index tracks the UK's most significant and record-breaking severe weather events, with full write-ups for the most notable ones.

Record-Breaking Events

The North Sea Flood

· January 31 - February 1, 1953

A catastrophic storm surge breached sea defences along the east coast, killing 307 people — Britain's deadliest peacetime natural disaster of the 20th century — and led directly to the Thames Barrier. Read the full story →

The Great Storm of 1987

· October 15-16, 1987

An exceptionally deep windstorm killed 18 people in England and felled an estimated 15 million trees overnight — still the reference point for the UK's most damaging windstorm. Read the full story →

The Birmingham Tornado

· July 28, 2005

A T4/T5 tornado tracked directly through several Birmingham neighbourhoods, damaging roughly 1,000 buildings without a single fatality. Read the full story →

The Boscastle Flood

· August 16, 2004

A catastrophic flash flood swept through a Cornish village in under an hour, sweeping away 75 cars — yet a mass helicopter rescue meant zero deaths. Read the full story →

The Somerset Levels Floods

· Winter 2013-14

England's wettest winter in roughly 250 years left the Somerset Levels underwater for weeks and reshaped UK flood policy. Read the full story →

Other Notable Events

  • London (Kensal Rise) tornado (December 7, 2006) — a T4 tornado tore through northwest London, dramatically filmed by residents, with no fatalities. Read the full story →
  • Burns' Day Storm (January 25, 1990) — a major windstorm that killed 47 people across the UK, deadlier than the Great Storm of 1987. Read the full story →
  • The Big Freeze (winter 1962-63) — the UK's coldest, most prolonged winter since 1740, with snow lying continuously for around two months. Read the full story →
  • Beast from the East (February-March 2018) — an extreme cold snap and blizzard that killed at least 17 people and paralyzed transport nationwide. Read the full story →
  • Storm Eunice (February 2022) — recorded England's highest-ever gust, 122 mph at the Isle of Wight, and killed several people across the UK. Read the full story →
  • Lynmouth flood (August 1952) — a devastating flash flood swept through the Devon village of Lynmouth after extreme rainfall on Exmoor, killing 34 people. Read the full story →
  • The Great Smog of London (December 1952) — a windless anticyclone and temperature inversion trapped coal smoke over London for five days, killing thousands and leading to the Clean Air Act. Read the full story →
  • The 1976 heatwave and drought — 15 consecutive days above 32°C on top of a 16-month drought, forcing standpipes into British streets. Read the full story →
  • The Fastnet Race storm (August 1979) — an unforecast storm devastated the offshore yacht racing fleet in the Irish Sea, killing 15 sailors. Read the full story →
  • Storm Ophelia (October 2017) — an ex-hurricane battered Ireland and the UK, and turned British skies orange with Saharan dust. Read the full story →
  • Cumbria floods, Cockermouth (November 2009) — record 24-hour rainfall for the UK at the time, one police officer killed.
  • Storm Desmond (December 2015) — set a new UK 24-hour rainfall record in Cumbria and caused widespread flooding across northern England and Scotland. Read the full story →
  • The Braer Storm (January 1993) — the deepest area of low pressure ever recorded in the North Atlantic, battering Shetland with hurricane-force winds. Read the full story →
  • The Great Storm of 1703 — the worst storm in Britain's recorded history, sinking much of the Royal Navy and killing thousands. Read the full story →
  • The July 2022 heat record — the UK exceeded 40°C for the first time, reaching 40.3°C at Coningsby. Read the full story →
  • Hull and East Yorkshire floods (June 2007) — surface water flooding hit around 8,600 Hull homes, in a disaster so overshadowed nationally that Hull called itself "the forgotten city." Read the full story →
  • Storm Arwen (November 2021) — an unusual northerly storm felled huge numbers of trees grown wind-firm against southwesterlies, leaving some homes without power for over a week. Read the full story →

Does El Niño Affect UK Weather?

Unlike Australia or New Zealand, where El Niño and La Niña have a strong, well-documented effect on rainfall and drought risk, the link between ENSO and UK weather is much weaker and more scientifically debated. Some research points to a modest tendency for El Niño winters to bring a more unsettled, stormier jet stream pattern to the UK, while La Niña winters show a weak tendency toward colder outbreaks — but the signal is far noisier than the Pacific basin's, and any individual UK winter is dominated far more by other factors, particularly the state of the North Atlantic Oscillation. Treat any specific "this winter will be X because of El Niño" claim for the UK with caution unless it comes directly from the Met Office.

How UK Severe Weather Is Rated and Named

Ireland shares the same Atlantic storm track and the same naming system — see the Ireland Severe Weather Index for its counterpart events.

Britain also records more tornadoes per unit area than almost any country on Earth — almost all of them too weak to notice. Here is why both halves of that are true.

Two reference guides sit behind most of the events on this page: British tornadoes are rated on the TORRO scale (T0-T11) rather than the American EF scale, and since 2015 UK storms have been given names under a joint naming system run with Ireland and the Netherlands.

Key Facts

  • TORRO, the UK's tornado research body, has documented well over 2,000 tornadoes across the British Isles since the 1600s — the UK ranks among the highest tornado-density countries in the world per unit area, though almost all are weak (T0-T3).
  • The UK's deadliest storms are almost always extratropical windstorms off the Atlantic, not tornadoes — the country's worst tornado disasters cause severe property damage but rarely mass casualties.
  • England experienced its wettest winter in roughly 250 years of record-keeping during 2013-14, directly causing the Somerset Levels floods.
  • The Met Office introduced a national storm-naming system (Storm Abigail onward, from 2015) partly in response to public confusion after major storms like the Great Storm of 1987 and Burns' Day Storm.

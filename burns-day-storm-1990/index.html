The Burns' Day Storm of 1990

Extratropical windstorm · United Kingdom · January 25, 1990 · 47 killed

On January 25, 1990 — Burns Night — an intense windstorm swept across England and Wales, killing 47 people in the UK. Despite being less famous than the Great Storm of 1987, the Burns' Day Storm was, in terms of lives lost, the deadlier of the two, because it struck during daylight on a weekday rather than overnight.

What Happened

An intense area of low pressure deepened rapidly over the Atlantic and crossed the UK on the morning of January 25, bringing widespread gusts of 80-90 mph across a huge swath of England and Wales, and a peak gust of around 107 mph recorded at Aberporth on the Welsh coast. Unlike the Great Storm of 1987, which struck in the early hours of the morning while most of the country slept, the Burns' Day Storm arrived during the working day.

Why It Was So Deadly

The timing proved decisive. With winds peaking mid-morning on a weekday, millions of people were already outdoors, driving to work, or walking through town centres and parks when trees began to fall and debris started flying. Most of the storm's 47 UK fatalities were caused by falling trees and flying debris striking people or vehicles — a very different casualty pattern from a storm that strikes an empty, sleeping country at night.

  • 47 fatalities in the UK, more than the Great Storm of 1987's 18
  • Widespread structural damage, downed trees, and power outages across England and Wales
  • Major disruption to road, rail, and air travel during the Friday morning commute
  • Insurance losses that ranked among the costliest UK storms of the era

A Less Remembered, But Deadlier, Storm

The Burns' Day Storm is often overshadowed in British popular memory by the more dramatically covered Great Storm of 1987, partly because 1987's storm was tied to the famous Michael Fish forecast moment. Meteorologists and emergency planners, however, frequently point to 1990 as the more instructive case study, precisely because its higher death toll illustrates how much the time of day a storm strikes can matter as much as its raw wind speed.

Lessons for Storm Warnings

The storm reinforced a lesson UK forecasters have carried into the modern storm-naming and warning system: a daytime windstorm with people outdoors and on the roads can be more dangerous, life for life, than a stronger nighttime storm. Modern UK severe weather warnings explicitly factor in the timing of peak winds relative to rush hour and school run periods for exactly this reason.

Aftermath

Clean-up and insurance claims from the Burns' Day Storm took months, and the event remains part of the standard reference set — alongside the Great Storm of 1987 and, more recently, Storm Eunice — that UK meteorologists cite when explaining just how destructive a UK windstorm can become.

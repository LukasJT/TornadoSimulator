The Thredbo Landslide of 1997

On the night of July 30, 1997, a sudden landslide swept down a slope above Thredbo, a ski resort town in the New South Wales Snowy Mountains, destroying two ski lodges in seconds and killing 18 people. The disaster became one of the most dramatic and closely followed rescue operations in modern Australian history.

What Happened

Thredbo had experienced several days of unusually heavy winter rainfall in the lead-up to the disaster, saturating an already unstable slope above the town's Bimbadeen and Carinya lodges, both of which housed ski resort staff. On the night of July 30, the waterlogged slope gave way, sending a fast-moving mass of mud, rock, and debris directly into the two buildings below.

The lodges were destroyed almost instantly, and the debris also severed the gas main running beneath the road above, complicating the rescue operation with an ongoing fire risk in the hours that followed.

The Rescue of Stuart Diver

The disaster became a defining moment in Australian history when ski instructor Stuart Diver was found alive, trapped in a small air pocket beneath tonnes of rubble, approximately 65 hours after the slide struck. His rescue, broadcast live to a nation that had feared no one could have survived, remains one of the most widely remembered moments of any Australian natural disaster.

  • 18 fatalities, all residents of the two destroyed staff lodges
  • One survivor, Stuart Diver, rescued alive after 65 hours buried in rubble
  • A severed gas main complicated the rescue with fire risk
  • One of the largest emergency response operations in New South Wales history

The Investigation

A subsequent coronial inquiry examined multiple potential contributing factors, including the days of heavy natural rainfall and a water main running beneath the Alpine Way road above the lodges, which some investigators suggested may have leaked and further saturated the slope. The inquiry did not find evidence of criminal negligence, and the disaster was ultimately attributed to a natural slope failure driven primarily by the exceptional rainfall.

A Distinctly Alpine Australian Disaster

Thredbo stands apart from most of the natural disasters commonly associated with Australia — cyclones, bushfires, and hailstorms — as a reminder that Australia's alpine regions carry their own distinct severe-weather-driven risks, including landslides triggered by heavy winter rain saturating steep terrain, a hazard more commonly associated with mountainous regions overseas.

Aftermath

Thredbo rebuilt its damaged lodges with updated geotechnical safeguards, and the disaster prompted broader reviews of slope stability assessment and building placement in Australia's alpine resort areas. The rescue of Stuart Diver remains one of the most enduring images in modern Australian disaster history, a rare moment of hope recovered from an otherwise devastating tragedy.

A History of Tornadoes in Miami

How Miami's geography shapes tornado risk, with important storms from the Miami historical record.

Scope: This article covers Miami and its surrounding metropolitan area. Tornado tracks often cross city, county, state or provincial boundaries.

How geography shapes the risk

Miami occupies a warm, low coastal plain between the Atlantic Ocean and Everglades. That setting does not create tornadoes by itself. It influences where boundaries form, how storms approach and which parts of the metropolitan area are exposed as a circulation crosses from one jurisdiction to another.

The recurring weather setup is straightforward: sea-breeze collisions, tropical rain bands and waterspouts moving ashore can create brief tornadoes. Tornado risk depends on the ingredients present on a particular day, so a city’s past is context rather than a forecast.

Core season

possible in every month, with peaks tied to summer storms and tropical systems.

Area covered

This guide uses “Miami” for the city and surrounding commuting region; event paths do not stop at municipal lines.

Important tornadoes in the Miami region

1925

Miami tornado

An April tornado damaged parts of the young city during south Florida’s early development era.

1992

Hurricane Andrew tornado threat

Andrew’s compact circulation brought extreme wind and embedded rotation to southern Miami-Dade County.

1997

Downtown Miami tornado

A photogenic May tornado crossed near downtown and Biscayne Bay, becoming one of the city’s most recognizable weather images.

What the record can and cannot tell us

Older tornado records favor destructive storms that crossed settled areas. Short-lived rural tornadoes were easier to miss before dense radar, trained spotter networks and widespread cameras. Modern databases are more complete, but ratings still describe surveyed damage rather than a direct measurement of peak wind at every point.

For local research, search the official event database by county or province and date, then read the responsible forecast office’s storm survey. This avoids a common mistake: assigning every tornado reported near Miami to the city proper.

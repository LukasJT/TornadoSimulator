Weather in poetry

Poets understand weather in ways scientists sometimes miss. Here are the poems that get storms right — from Dickinson to modern voices.

Emily Dickinson

Dickinson wrote weather constantly. Some highlights:

  • "There came a Wind like a Bugle" — probably about a tornado approach.
  • "A narrow Wind complains all day" — the sound of wind through eaves.
  • "The Sky is low — the Clouds are mean" — flat gray sky.
  • Repeated imagery of storms, wind, weather-as-emotion.
  • Amherst, Massachusetts landscape.
  • Modern chase-related poetry frequently references her.

Ted Kooser

Nebraska poet laureate. Great Plains weather is his territory.

  • "After Rain" — sensory landscape.
  • "Weather" — brief but evocative.
  • "Winter Morning" — cold air, low sun.
  • Understated, precise, plainspoken.
  • Farm and small-town setting.
  • Read by chasers who spend seasons in his territory.

Wallace Stevens

Insurance executive and modernist master. Weather as metaphysical.

  • "The Snow Man" — cold vision.
  • "Anecdote of the Jar" — placing weather in landscape.
  • "Sunday Morning" — light and season.
  • "The Idea of Order at Key West" — beach weather.
  • Weather as inescapable presence.

Robert Frost

  • "Storm Fear" — a family confronts a snowstorm.
  • "After Apple-Picking" — autumn cold.
  • "Once by the Pacific" — coastal storm.
  • "Fire and Ice" — apocalyptic imagery.
  • "The Rain to the Wind Said" — weather dialogue.
  • New England setting.

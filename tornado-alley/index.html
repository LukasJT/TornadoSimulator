What Is Tornado Alley?

Tornado Alley is the central US region with the highest tornado frequency in the world — Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas, Nebraska, and parts of the Dakotas. Boundaries, statistics, and Dixie Alley compared.

Which States Are In Tornado Alley?

Because there is no official definition, meteorologists disagree on the exact boundaries. The traditional core — as originally described by US Air Force meteorologists in 1952 — includes:

  • Texas — especially the Panhandle and North Texas plains
  • Oklahoma — the heart of the traditional Alley
  • Kansas — highest tornadoes-per-square-mile of any state most years
  • Nebraska
  • South Dakota (southern portion)

Broader definitions extend into Colorado, Iowa, Missouri, and parts of Minnesota.

Why This Region?

Tornado Alley sits at the collision zone of three distinct air masses that meet more reliably here than anywhere else on Earth:

  • Warm, moist air flowing north from the Gulf of Mexico
  • Cool, dry air descending from the Rocky Mountains and Canadian Prairies
  • Hot, dry air flowing off the Mexican Plateau and Southwest deserts

When these air masses collide during spring and early summer, they produce the wind shear and instability that generates supercell thunderstorms — the parent storms of nearly all violent (EF3+) tornadoes. Nowhere else on Earth combines these three air masses over such flat terrain, which allows storms to organize into large, long-lived supercells.

Tornado Alley vs. Dixie Alley

Since the 2000s, meteorologists have increasingly recognized a second high-tornado region: Dixie Alley, covering Mississippi, Alabama, Louisiana, Arkansas, Tennessee, and parts of Georgia. Dixie Alley produces fewer tornadoes per year than Tornado Alley — but on average, its tornadoes are deadlier.

Why Dixie Alley tornadoes kill more people per event:

  • Nighttime tornadoes — Dixie Alley has 2–3× the nocturnal tornado rate of Tornado Alley, and nighttime tornadoes kill 2.5× more people (Ashley, 2007).
  • More mobile homes — Southeast US has the country's highest concentration of manufactured housing, which offers little protection at EF2+.
  • Poorer visibility — Dixie tornadoes are often rain-wrapped and harder to see than the classic Great Plains "wedge."
  • Denser population — the Southeast has more people per square mile than the western Plains.

The 2011 Super Outbreak — which produced Tuscaloosa 2011, Hackleburg 2011, and Smithville 2011 — hit Dixie Alley squarely.

Is Tornado Alley Shifting East?

Research published since 2018 (Gensini & Brooks, 2018 among others) shows the highest tornado frequency has been gradually shifting east since the 1980s — away from the traditional Tornado Alley core in the western Plains and toward the Mid-South and Dixie Alley states. Reasons include:

  • Warmer Gulf of Mexico waters supplying more moisture to eastern storms
  • Reduced tornado frequency over Texas and Oklahoma as the desert Southwest expands north
  • More favorable wind shear patterns in the Mid-South during peak season

Whether this represents a permanent climate-driven shift or a short-term fluctuation is still actively debated.

Tornado Alley Cities to Know

  • Oklahoma City / Moore, OK — struck by major tornadoes in 1999, 2003, and 2013. See Bridge Creek-Moore 1999 and Moore 2013.
  • Dallas–Fort Worth, TX — struck by significant tornadoes in 2000, 2015, and 2019.
  • Wichita, KS — near the path of the 1991 Andover F5 and the 2022 Andover EF3.
  • Joplin, MO — 2011 EF5, deadliest single US tornado since 1947. Full story →
  • Topeka, KS — 1966 F5.
  • Woodward, OK — 1947 F5, one of the deadliest US tornadoes.
  • Greensburg, KS — 2007 EF5, 95% of town destroyed. Full story →

Tornado Alley Season

Peak tornado activity in Tornado Alley runs from April through June, with a secondary peak in November for parts of the southern Plains. Roughly 75% of all US tornadoes occur between March and July. Dixie Alley has a bimodal season — spring (March–May) and fall (October–December).

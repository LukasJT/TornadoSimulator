El Niño and La Niña — What They Do to Australian Weather

Few countries feel the swing of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) as strongly as Australia. The cycle's two opposite phases — El Niño and La Niña — are linked to some of the biggest swings in the country's severe weather risk, from drought and catastrophic bushfire seasons to some of its worst flooding disasters.

What Is ENSO?

ENSO describes a cycle of warming and cooling in sea surface temperatures across the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean, which shifts every few years between three states: El Niño (warmer than normal), La Niña (cooler than normal), and neutral conditions in between. These Pacific temperature shifts change where and how much rain falls across a huge swath of the globe, and Australia — sitting on the western edge of the Pacific — is one of the most strongly affected countries on Earth.

El Niño in Australia: Drought and Fire Risk

During El Niño, the trade winds that normally push warm water and moisture toward Australia weaken, and rainfall shifts east across the Pacific instead. For eastern and northern Australia, this typically means:

  • Below-average rainfall across much of eastern Australia, particularly in winter and spring
  • Reduced soil moisture and lower stream flows heading into the fire season
  • Above-average bushfire danger across southeastern Australia, since dry fuel is the single biggest driver of fire risk
  • An increased chance of heatwaves, since drier soil heats up faster under sunny skies

El Niño conditions are frequently cited as a contributing factor in some of Australia's most severe bushfire seasons, since dry, drought-stressed fuel loads are one of the necessary ingredients for the most extreme fire behaviour, alongside heat and wind on the day itself.

La Niña in Australia: Flood Risk

La Niña reverses the pattern: stronger trade winds push more warm, moist air toward Australia, typically bringing:

  • Above-average rainfall across much of eastern Australia
  • Higher river and catchment levels heading into the wet season
  • Increased risk of major flooding, since saturated catchments respond more violently to additional heavy rain
  • A more active tropical cyclone season in some years, though the relationship with individual cyclones is more complex than with rainfall

The 2010-11 Queensland floods, including the Toowoomba "inland tsunami," struck during one of the strongest La Niña events on record — widely cited as a major contributing factor to how saturated Queensland's catchments already were before the January 2011 rain fell.

A Shifted Probability, Not a Guarantee

It's important to understand what ENSO does and doesn't predict: an El Niño or La Niña declaration shifts the odds toward drier or wetter conditions across the affected regions, it does not guarantee a specific outcome. Plenty of El Niño years have still produced significant rain events in parts of Australia, and plenty of La Niña years have had drier-than-expected stretches in some regions. Local factors — soil moisture, individual weather systems, and other climate drivers like the Indian Ocean Dipole — all layer on top of the broader ENSO signal.

How to Track It

The Bureau of Meteorology issues regular ENSO Wrap-Up updates, tracking Pacific sea surface temperatures, trade wind strength, and the Southern Oscillation Index (the pressure difference between Darwin and Tahiti) to classify current conditions and issue seasonal outlooks. These updates are the standard reference for understanding Australia's shifting drought and flood risk through any given year.

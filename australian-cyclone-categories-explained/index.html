Australian Cyclone Categories Explained

Why comparing an Australian Category 4 to a US Category 4 directly will mislead you.

This is one of the most persistent sources of confusion in international weather reporting: an Australian Category 5 cyclone and an American Category 5 hurricane are not measured the same way, and treating the numbers as equivalent will mislead you.

The Core Difference: Gust vs Sustained

The Australian scale, used by the Bureau of Meteorology, categorises tropical cyclones by the strongest expected wind gust. The Saffir-Simpson scale, used in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific, categorises hurricanes by maximum sustained wind.

Gusts are always higher than sustained winds — typically by a substantial margin. So for the same physical storm, the gust-based Australian number will generally look more severe than the sustained-based American number would. A direct "Category 4 equals Category 4" comparison across the two systems systematically overstates the Australian storm.

The Australian Categories

Approximate wind gust ranges on the Australian scale:

  • Category 1 — gusts under about 125 km/h. Minor damage, some crop and tree damage.
  • Category 2 — gusts around 125-164 km/h. Minor house damage, significant damage to crops and caravans.
  • Category 3 — gusts around 165-224 km/h. Roof and structural damage; classified as a severe tropical cyclone from this point up.
  • Category 4 — gusts around 225-279 km/h. Significant roofing loss and structural damage, dangerous airborne debris.
  • Category 5 — gusts above about 280 km/h. Extremely dangerous, widespread destruction.

Note that Australia uses the term "severe tropical cyclone" for Category 3 and above — the rough equivalent of the American "major hurricane" designation.

The Averaging Period Problem

There is a second, compounding difference. When sustained wind is quoted, Australia and most of the world use a 10-minute averaging period, while the United States uses 1 minute. A shorter averaging window captures more of the peaks, so the same storm yields a higher sustained-wind figure under the US convention.

The practical consequence: international comparisons of tropical cyclone intensity require converting between conventions, and headline numbers quoted across borders are frequently not like-for-like.

What This Looks Like in Practice

Cyclone Tracy (1974) and Cyclone Yasi (2011) are both described in Australian terms, on the Australian scale. Yasi made landfall as an Australian Category 5 — a genuinely extreme storm — but reading that as "equivalent to a Saffir-Simpson Category 5 Atlantic hurricane" is not correct, even though it was undoubtedly a very powerful system by any measure.

What Actually Matters for Safety

For anyone in the path, the category number is far less useful than the specific local warnings: expected storm surge height, rainfall totals, and evacuation instructions. Category conveys wind severity only, and in many cyclones — Debbie being a clear example — the deadliest impacts came from flooding well after the winds had eased.

Tornado Debris Signature: What Radar Is Showing

A plain-language guide to tornado debris signatures on dual-polarization radar and what they can and cannot tell you.

Quick answer
A tornado debris signature is a radar pattern consistent with debris being lofted into the air at the same place as strong rotation. It is powerful evidence of a damaging tornado, but it is not a direct EF rating.

The ingredients on radar

Meteorologists look for tight velocity rotation and dual-polarization data showing objects that do not behave like uniform raindrops or hail. When those signals overlap, confidence rises that debris is airborne.

The common correlation coefficient product helps distinguish similarly shaped precipitation from a mixture of irregular objects. The signature may appear as a compact area near the circulation.

What it confirms

A convincing debris signature can provide radar confirmation when darkness, rain, or sparse spotter coverage hides the tornado. Warning text may then use “radar confirmed” or observed language.

Detection depends on radar distance, beam height, debris lofting height, terrain, storm structure, and the amount and type of material available. Absence of the signature does not prove there is no tornado.

What it cannot rate

The Enhanced Fujita Scale is assigned after survey teams examine damage indicators and degrees of damage. A larger or more intense-looking radar signature does not translate directly to an EF number.

During a warning, the useful interpretation is simple: a debris signature supports immediate confidence that damaging circulation is occurring.

If a warning includes you

  1. Move to the best available shelter immediately.
  2. Get low and protect your head and neck from debris.
  3. Keep monitoring official updates from shelter.

Frequently asked questions

Is the debris ball visible on reflectivity?

Sometimes high reflectivity appears near the hook, but meteorologists confirm the interpretation with velocity and dual-polarization products.

Can leaves create a signature?

Biological scatterers can affect radar, but operational interpretation requires spatial and meteorological context, especially collocation with strong rotation.

Does every tornado have one?

No. Weak, brief, distant, or rural tornadoes may not loft detectable debris high enough for the radar beam.

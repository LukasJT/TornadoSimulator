The UK Heat Record of July 2022

Extreme heat · United Kingdom · July 18-19, 2022 · 40.3°C, an all-time record

On July 19, 2022, the United Kingdom exceeded 40°C for the first time in its recorded history, with a peak of 40.3°C at Coningsby, Lincolnshire. The Met Office had issued the country's first-ever red extreme heat warning, and the day saw rail lines buckle, runways affected, and the London Fire Brigade face its busiest day since the Second World War.

A Record Broken by a Wide Margin

National temperature records usually fall by fractions of a degree. July 2022 broke the UK's previous record of 38.7°C — set at Cambridge in 2019 — by roughly 1.6°C, and dozens of stations across England exceeded the old record on the same day. Exceeding 40°C had, until then, been regarded as something the UK climate could produce only rarely if at all.

Infrastructure Under Strain

The UK's built environment is engineered for a temperate maritime climate, and 40°C exposed that mismatch comprehensively. Rail operators imposed severe speed restrictions or cancelled services outright because track is stressed for lower maximum temperatures and can buckle; some rail infrastructure caught fire. Road surfaces softened in places, and Luton Airport briefly suspended flights after a runway surface issue.

  • 40.3°C recorded at Coningsby, Lincolnshire — a new UK all-time record
  • The first red extreme heat warning ever issued by the Met Office
  • Widespread rail cancellations and speed restrictions
  • The London Fire Brigade's busiest day since the Second World War, driven by grass and property fires
  • A significant excess death toll, concentrated among older and vulnerable people

The Wildfires

One of the most striking features of the day was fire. Tinder-dry grassland around London ignited repeatedly, and fires spread into residential areas in several locations, destroying homes. The scale of simultaneous incidents forced the London Fire Brigade to declare a major incident, in a scenario British fire services had rarely had to plan for at that intensity.

2022 Versus 1976

The UK's two benchmark hot-weather events have opposite profiles. 1976 was about duration: weeks of sustained heat on top of a 16-month drought, producing standpipes and a national water emergency. 2022 was about peak intensity: a short, extraordinarily hot spike that broke the national record decisively and stressed physical infrastructure, without the same prolonged water crisis. Both are now standard reference points, describing two genuinely different failure modes for a country built around mild summers.

What Changed After

July 2022 drove significant follow-up work on UK heat resilience — rail network heat tolerance, building overheating standards, health service heat planning, and the public warning system itself. It also permanently changed the framing of British heat: 40°C moved from a hypothetical to a documented occurrence, which is a materially different basis for infrastructure planning.

Miller A vs. Miller B nor’easters: where does the second low come from?

Follow where the first low forms and when a coastal low takes over in a Miller A or Miller B snowstorm.

Tornado Hub · November 21, 2026

A Miller A storm develops near the Gulf or Southeast and moves up the East Coast as the main low. In a Miller B setup, a primary low approaches from inland and a new circulation develops near the coast. Forecasters track both pressure centers during the transfer.

Trace both centers on the same map

Look for closed isobars and the analyzed surface low positions. On consecutive maps, follow the original system and note when a separate closed circulation appears near the coast. NWS State College’s storm-type diagrams show the two idealized tracks.

Why the transfer matters

Atlantic moisture wraps toward the cold side of the new center. A transfer farther south can bring that moisture into an inland cold layer sooner, while later redevelopment can shift the strongest snow band north or east. The timing also affects places caught between the inland and coastal precipitation shields.

Work an analysis example

Imagine a 12 UTC analysis shows a low in Ohio. At 18 UTC the Ohio center weakens and a second low closes east of Virginia. At 00 UTC the coastal center is deeper and moving northeast. This is a schematic Miller B transfer. To assess snowfall in a specific town, check the temperature profile and where sustained ascent overlaps the cold air.

The Perryton, Texas Tornado of June 15, 2023

On the evening of June 15, 2023, an EF3 tornado tore through Perryton, Texas, a small city of roughly 8,000 people in the northeastern Texas Panhandle. The tornado struck during the dinner-hour rush, killing three people and injuring around 100 more — the deadliest single tornado to hit the Texas Panhandle in years.

What Happened

The tornado formed as part of a broader severe weather outbreak that raked the southern Plains that afternoon and evening, with numerous tornado warnings issued across the Texas and Oklahoma Panhandles. The Perryton tornado touched down on the edge of town in the early evening and tracked through populated areas, catching residents who were driving home from work, at local businesses, or already at home.

National Weather Service damage survey teams rated the tornado EF3, with winds estimated up to around 140 mph. The path cut across a mix of residential streets, mobile home lots, and commercial buildings along the edge of the city.

Damage and Impact

The worst damage was concentrated in and around a mobile home community, where structures offer little protection against EF3-strength winds. Dozens of homes were destroyed or badly damaged, vehicles were tossed and mangled, and trees and power lines came down across the city. Local businesses along the highway corridor sustained heavy structural damage as well.

  • Three fatalities, with most or all of the deaths concentrated in the mobile home community
  • Approximately 100 injuries reported in the immediate aftermath
  • Widespread destruction of manufactured and site-built homes along the tornado's path
  • Significant damage to local businesses and infrastructure near the city's commercial corridor

Why Mobile Homes Were So Vulnerable

Perryton's tragedy is a familiar pattern in US tornado climatology: manufactured and mobile homes are dramatically more likely to be destroyed — and to produce fatalities — than anchored, site-built homes at the same wind speed. An EF3 tornado can flip or roll an unanchored mobile home well before it would seriously threaten a well-built house, which is why national safety guidance is unusually blunt on this point: mobile home residents should identify a sturdy shelter location before severe weather arrives, since a mobile home itself is never considered safe shelter, even tied down.

The Broader Plains Outbreak

The Perryton tornado was one of several significant tornadoes that touched down across the Texas and Oklahoma Panhandles that evening as a potent supercell cluster moved across the region. The Storm Prediction Center had highlighted the area for a heightened severe weather risk earlier in the day, and warnings were in place ahead of the tornado's arrival in Perryton — but the combination of a fast-developing, high-end tornado and a community with substantial mobile-home housing stock proved deadly regardless.

Aftermath

Perryton received a federal disaster declaration in the tornado's aftermath, unlocking assistance for individuals and the local government. Community organizations, regional volunteer groups, and Panhandle-area emergency management coordinated debris removal and temporary housing support in the following weeks. As in many small Plains and Dixie Alley towns hit by high-end tornadoes, some displaced mobile-home residents faced a longer road to permanent housing than homeowners with insured, site-built homes.

The event renewed a familiar conversation in tornado-prone rural communities about the gap between how common mobile and manufactured housing is across the Plains and how few of those homes have nearby, accessible storm shelters.

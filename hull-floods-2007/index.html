How Hull described it

In June 2007, roughly 8,600 homes flooded in Hull — one of the highest totals for any UK city in a summer of exceptional flooding across England. Yet the event is barely remembered nationally, and Hull's own council came to describe it as "the forgotten city" flood.

What Happened

Summer 2007 was extraordinarily wet across England. On 25 June, an intense rainfall event hit East Yorkshire on ground already saturated by weeks of preceding rain. Hull's drainage system was overwhelmed within hours.

Critically, this was not river flooding. Water did not arrive from the Humber or the River Hull bursting their banks. It was rainfall that simply could not drain away — surface water flooding, sometimes called pluvial flooding, and one of the least well-understood flood mechanisms in the UK.

Why Hull Is Especially Vulnerable

Most of Hull sits at or below high tide level. That means the city cannot simply let water run downhill to the sea — it depends on an extensive network of drains and pumping stations to lift water out.

A pumped drainage system has a hard ceiling. Once rainfall exceeds pumping capacity, water accumulates with nowhere to go, and it keeps accumulating until the rain stops. That is precisely what happened in June 2007, and it is why the flooding was so widespread across the city rather than confined to a floodplain.

  • Around 8,600 homes flooded across the city
  • Roughly 1,300 businesses affected
  • One man died in a drain while attempting to clear a blockage
  • Nearly every school in the city sustained some damage
  • Thousands of families in temporary accommodation, many for a year or more

"The Forgotten City"

The summer 2007 floods hit large parts of England, and national coverage concentrated overwhelmingly on Gloucestershire, Tewkesbury and Sheffield — helped by dramatic imagery like the Mythe water treatment works being surrounded and a power station under threat.

Hull, with a higher household flood count than most of those places, received comparatively little attention. The phrase "the forgotten city" came out of Hull's own review of the disaster and stuck, becoming a recurring case study in how disaster media coverage is driven by imagery and narrative rather than by scale of impact.

What It Changed

The 2007 floods nationally — Hull included — drove the Pitt Review, a major independent examination of English flood risk management. One of its central conclusions was that surface water flooding had been seriously underestimated relative to river and coastal flooding, which had historically absorbed most attention and funding.

That shift in emphasis, toward mapping and managing surface water risk in urban areas, is among the most consequential outcomes of the 2007 floods, and Hull's experience was central evidence for it.

The Broader Lesson

Hull illustrates something that recurs across UK flood events: the flooding that gets attention is not necessarily the flooding that does the most damage. Dramatic river breaches photograph well. Thousands of homes filling with drain water across a low-lying city, gradually, over a day, does not — and yet it displaced more households than most of what led the bulletins.

The Black Summer Bushfires (2019-20)

The Australian bushfire season of 2019-20 — universally known as Black Summer — burned an unprecedented area of eastern Australia over months rather than days, destroyed more than 3,000 homes, killed 33 people directly, and subjected millions of city residents to hazardous smoke for weeks at a time.

A Season, Not a Day

Australia's most infamous fire disasters — Black Saturday and Ash Wednesday — were single catastrophic days, where extreme fire weather produced enormous loss of life within hours. Black Summer had a fundamentally different shape. Fires were burning in Queensland and northern New South Wales by winter and early spring 2019, months before the conventional fire season, and continued through to rain in February and March 2020.

The preceding period had been exceptionally dry and hot across eastern Australia, leaving forest and grassland fuel critically dry over an enormous area simultaneously. That set the conditions for fires that, once started, could not be contained and simply kept burning for months.

The Scale

The total area burned across the season ran to tens of millions of hectares nationally — an area without modern precedent in Australian records. Entire communities on the New South Wales south coast, in East Gippsland in Victoria, and on Kangaroo Island in South Australia were devastated, in some cases with residents evacuated to beaches as the only defensible ground remaining.

  • 33 direct fatalities, including several volunteer firefighters
  • More than 3,000 homes destroyed
  • Tens of millions of hectares burned across eastern and southern Australia
  • Enormous wildlife losses, with estimates running into the billions of animals affected
  • Naval and military evacuations of coastal communities cut off by fire

The Smoke Emergency

One of Black Summer's most distinctive features was the sheer scale of the smoke. Sydney, Canberra, and Melbourne spent extended periods under smoke producing air quality readings among the worst in the world, with Canberra at times recording the single worst air quality of any major city globally. Public health research has since attributed several hundred additional deaths to prolonged smoke exposure — meaning smoke, not flame, was the season's largest killer.

This made Black Summer a landmark event in how Australia understands bushfire risk: not only as a threat to those in the fire's path, but as a large-scale public health emergency affecting millions of people hundreds of kilometres from any fire front.

The Royal Commission

The season was followed by a national Royal Commission into Natural Disaster Arrangements, which examined federal and state coordination, aerial firefighting capacity, evacuation and warning systems, and how Australia prepares for fire seasons that begin earlier and last longer. Its findings drove changes to national disaster coordination arrangements and firefighting resource sharing between states.

Aftermath

Recovery across affected communities took years, complicated by the arrival of the COVID-19 pandemic almost immediately after the fires ended. Black Summer is now the reference event for a compound, season-long Australian fire emergency, discussed alongside Black Saturday as the two poles of Australian bushfire disaster — one a single catastrophic afternoon, the other an entire summer that would not end.

The Lynmouth Flood of 1952

Flash flood · Lynmouth, Devon · August 15-16, 1952 · 34 killed

On the night of August 15-16, 1952, the small Devon coastal village of Lynmouth was devastated when torrential rain over Exmoor sent a torrent of water, boulders, and debris down the East and West Lyn rivers directly into the village centre. Thirty-four people were killed and around a hundred buildings were destroyed or badly damaged in a single night.

What Happened

August 1952 had already been unusually wet across southwest England, leaving the peat and soil of Exmoor — the high moorland behind Lynmouth — thoroughly saturated and unable to absorb further rainfall. On August 15, an intense rainfall event dropped roughly 230mm of rain on the moor in 24 hours. With the ground unable to take any of it, virtually all of that water ran straight off the surface into the river system.

The East and West Lyn rivers descend steeply from Exmoor through narrow, confined valleys and converge at Lynmouth, right where the village sits at sea level. That topography concentrated the runoff into a violent, fast-moving surge rather than the gradual rise a lowland river would produce.

Debris, Boulders, and Bridge Dams

What made Lynmouth exceptionally destructive was not water alone. The torrent picked up enormous boulders, thousands of tonnes of gravel, and huge quantities of felled trees as it descended. This debris jammed against the village's bridges, forming temporary dams that impounded water behind them before catastrophically bursting — sending successive surges through the village and, in places, forcing the river to carve an entirely new course through the streets.

  • 34 fatalities, one of England's deadliest modern flash floods
  • Around 100 buildings destroyed or seriously damaged
  • Boulders weighing many tonnes deposited in the village centre
  • Bridges destroyed and the river's course permanently altered

Rebuilding a Village

Lynmouth was substantially rebuilt over the following years, with the river channel through the village significantly widened and deepened to carry a far greater flow, and the river's route through the village reconfigured. Those works remain visible in the village's layout today, and it is one of the clearest examples in Britain of a settlement physically redesigned around the flood that destroyed it.

A Recurring British Pattern

Lynmouth sits alongside the Boscastle flood of 2004 as one of the two defining British flash flood disasters, and the parallels between them are striking: a steep coastal catchment, extreme localized rainfall onto saturated ground, water funnelled into a narrow valley containing a village at the outflow, and debris turning a flood into something closer to a landslide of water and rock. Boscastle's zero death toll, half a century later, is largely down to daylight timing and modern helicopter rescue — not to any lesser violence in the flood itself.

The Great Snow of 2011

In mid-August 2011, an exceptionally powerful outbreak of Antarctic air brought snow to parts of New Zealand that almost never see it, including brief flurries reported around the wider Auckland region — normally far too mild for snow — and the heaviest snowfall Wellington had experienced in decades.

What Happened

An unusually intense and far-reaching outbreak of cold Antarctic air pushed north across New Zealand from around August 14, bringing snow to unusually low elevations and unusually northern latitudes for several consecutive days. Wellington, the capital, received its heaviest snow in decades, while further south the South Island's already snow-prone regions saw significant additional accumulation.

The most remarkable aspect of the event was how far north the cold air and snow reached: brief flurries were reported in parts of the greater Auckland region, a subtropical part of the North Island where snow at low elevation is an extraordinarily rare occurrence, drawing national and even international media attention.

Damage and Impact

Snow and ice disrupted roads, flights, and schools across large parts of the North and South Islands for several days. Wellington's usually hilly, wind-exposed streets became hazardous, and numerous flights in and out of the capital and other affected centres were cancelled or delayed as airports dealt with snow and ice on runways.

  • Wellington's heaviest snowfall in decades
  • Rare snow flurries reported around the greater Auckland region
  • Widespread road, rail, and flight disruption across the North and South Islands
  • School closures across multiple regions for several days

Why It Was So Unusual

New Zealand's North Island, and Auckland in particular, sits at a subtropical enough latitude and close enough to the surrounding ocean that snow at low elevation is exceptionally uncommon, even in a cold southerly outbreak. The scale of the August 2011 event — pushing genuinely cold Antarctic air that far north with enough moisture to produce snow — made it one of the most unusual widely documented cold-weather events in modern New Zealand history.

A Reference Cold Event

MetService and New Zealand climatologists still cite the August 2011 event as the benchmark for how far north and how disruptive a New Zealand cold outbreak can become, useful context whenever a significant southerly change is forecast for the country, even though a repeat of snow reaching Auckland's latitude remains a rare occurrence.

Aftermath

Once the cold outbreak cleared after several days, New Zealand's transport networks and schools returned to normal relatively quickly, and no significant loss of life was attributed to the event. It is remembered less for its destructiveness than for how visibly it demonstrated the reach of an unusually powerful Antarctic outbreak across a country not built around dealing with snow at its northern latitudes.

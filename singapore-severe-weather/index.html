Severe Weather in Singapore

Sitting almost on the equator, Singapore has no winters, no dry season, and — remarkably — almost no tropical cyclones. What it does have is some of the most intense convective weather on Earth: violent morning squalls, world-leading lightning, flash floods, and a warming, hazier future. Here is the whole picture.

Start here

Singapore lies about 1.3 degrees north of the equator. That single geographic fact shapes everything about its weather: relentless heat and humidity, daily thunderstorm potential, two monsoon seasons instead of four temperate ones, and near-immunity from the typhoons that batter countries just a few hundred kilometres to the north.

This guide breaks the island's severe weather into the hazards that actually matter here, each with its own in-depth page.

The hazards, explained

Sumatra squalls

The signature Singapore storm: a line of thunderstorms that forms overnight over Sumatra, sweeps east across the Strait of Malacca, and slams the island in the early morning with sudden 40–80 km/h gusts, blinding rain, and constant lightning. This is what topples trees and floods roads.

Lightning

Singapore records among the highest rates of lightning activity anywhere on the planet, with thunder heard on a large share of days each year. It is a genuine and deadly hazard, and the country has built world-class detection and warning systems around it.

Flash flooding

Intense tropical downpours can dump a month's worth of rain in an afternoon. Even with one of the world's most advanced drainage networks, Singapore still sees flash floods — including the famous Orchard Road floods.

The two monsoons

Instead of spring, summer, autumn, and winter, Singapore cycles through the Northeast Monsoon, the Southwest Monsoon, and two inter-monsoon periods — each with its own storm personality.

Hurricanes and typhoons

Short answer: no direct hits, essentially ever — the physics near the equator forbid it. The one staggering exception, Typhoon Vamei in 2001, is one of the most remarkable events in tropical meteorology.

Tornadoes and waterspouts

Full-blown tornadoes are almost unheard of, but waterspouts spin up over the surrounding seas and occasionally come ashore. Here is what is possible and what is not.

Transboundary haze

Not a storm, but a recurring atmospheric emergency: smoke from regional land and peat fires that can push air quality into hazardous territory for weeks.

Extreme heat and climate change

Singapore is warming faster than the global average. Its record of 37.0°C, set in 1983, was matched in 2023 — and the humid heat here is uniquely dangerous.

Storm Ophelia (2017)

Ex-hurricane · Ireland & United Kingdom · October 16, 2017 · 3 killed in Ireland

On October 16, 2017, the remnants of Hurricane Ophelia — which had reached major hurricane strength further east in the Atlantic than almost any storm on record — battered Ireland and parts of the UK with winds gusting over 90 mph, killing 3 people in Ireland. The storm is also remembered for an eerie side effect: it turned the sky over parts of the UK a deep orange and red for much of the day.

What Happened

Ophelia intensified into a major Category 3 hurricane while still over the open Atlantic, at a latitude and longitude unusually far east and north for a storm of that strength — a genuine meteorological rarity. As it curved north toward Ireland and the UK, it weakened into an ex-hurricane / post-tropical storm, but still carried destructive winds gusting over 90 mph across parts of Ireland and southwest Britain.

The Orange Sky

Ahead of the storm, Ophelia's circulation drew a plume of Saharan dust, combined with smoke from severe wildfires burning in Spain and Portugal at the time, northward across the UK. The combination scattered sunlight in a way that turned skies over southern England, London, and other areas a striking deep orange and red for much of the day — an image that dominated British media coverage even before the storm's winds arrived.

Damage and Impact

Ireland bore the brunt of Ophelia's winds, with widespread power outages, downed trees, and significant disruption to transport. Three people were killed in Ireland, mostly in incidents involving falling trees. The UK also saw disruption, particularly across Wales and southwest England, though with a lower overall impact than Ireland.

  • 3 fatalities, all in Ireland
  • Winds gusting over 90 mph across parts of Ireland and southwest Britain
  • Widespread power outages across Ireland
  • An unusually vivid orange sky over parts of the UK, driven by Saharan dust and wildfire smoke

A Meteorological Rarity

Ophelia's peak intensity as a major hurricane so far east in the Atlantic drew significant attention from meteorologists, who noted it as one of the furthest-east major hurricanes on record in the Atlantic basin. Its transition into a powerful post-tropical storm that still delivered damaging winds to Ireland and the UK is frequently cited as an example of how former tropical systems can remain dangerous long after losing their tropical characteristics.

Aftermath

Ireland's electricity network took days to fully restore power to all affected areas, and the storm prompted renewed public discussion in both Ireland and the UK about preparedness for ex-tropical systems, which are less common and less anticipated by the public than the UK's more familiar winter windstorms.

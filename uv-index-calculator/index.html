What the UV index numbers mean

UV indexCategoryWhat to do
0–2LowNo protection needed for most people.
3–5ModerateSeek shade near midday; hat and sunscreen for extended time outside.
6–7HighProtection required. Reduce midday sun exposure.
8–10Very HighExtra protection. Unprotected skin burns quickly.
11+ExtremeTake all precautions. Avoid the sun midday.

How the burn-time estimate works

One UV index unit equals about 25 milliwatts per square metre of sunburning (erythemal) ultraviolet energy. Each skin type can absorb a certain dose before it reddens, called the minimal erythemal dose. Dividing that dose by the incoming UV energy gives an estimated time to burn. An SPF value multiplies that time by roughly its number: SPF 30 gives about 30 times longer, in theory.

Treat the result as a ceiling, not a promise. Real sunscreen protection is usually lower than the label because most people apply too little and reapply too rarely.

What raises your real exposure

Several things push the effective UV higher than the reported index. Snow reflects up to 80 percent of UV back at you, sand and water around 15 to 25 percent, so ski slopes and beaches burn faster. UV also climbs roughly 10 to 12 percent for every 1,000 metres of altitude. And thin cloud barely blocks UV — you can burn badly on an overcast day.

Protecting yourself

Shade, a wide-brimmed hat, UV-blocking sunglasses, and tightly woven clothing beat sunscreen alone. When you do use sunscreen, apply a generous layer 15 minutes before going out and reapply every two hours, and more often after swimming or sweating. The sun is strongest from about 10 a.m. to 4 p.m.

Note: This is a general estimate for clear midday sun and is not medical advice. Everyone's skin differs, and repeated tanning or burning at any skin type raises long-term skin-cancer risk.

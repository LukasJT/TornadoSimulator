The Beast from the East (2018)

Extreme cold outbreak · United Kingdom · February 22 - March 5, 2018 · At least 17 killed

From late February into early March 2018, an extreme cold outbreak nicknamed the Beast from the East brought record-low temperatures, severe blizzards, and the most disruptive winter weather the UK had experienced in years. At least 17 people were killed, and transport networks across the country were paralyzed for days.

What Happened

A sudden stratospheric warming event over the Arctic in early February 2018 disrupted the polar vortex, the band of cold air that normally circulates around the North Pole. That disruption allowed an exceptionally cold air mass to plunge south from Siberia across much of Europe, including the UK, from around February 22. Temperatures fell to between -11°C and -14°C in parts of the country — among the coldest readings recorded in decades.

As the cold snap settled in, Storm Emma arrived from the southwest on March 1-2, and the collision between the mild, moist Atlantic storm and the deeply entrenched cold air produced severe blizzard conditions, with snow drifts several metres deep in exposed parts of southern and southwestern England and Wales.

Damage and Impact

Roads across the country became impassable, with thousands of drivers stranded overnight in vehicles, including a widely reported incident on the A3 in Surrey where hundreds of motorists were trapped for over 10 hours. Rail services were suspended nationwide, hundreds of flights were cancelled, and schools and businesses closed across most of the UK for several days.

  • At least 17 fatalities linked to the cold and its effects
  • Temperatures as low as -11°C to -14°C in parts of the UK
  • Severe blizzards and multi-metre snow drifts where Storm Emma met the cold air
  • Widespread, multi-day disruption to road, rail, and air travel nationwide

Why It Caught the UK Off Guard

The UK's transport and energy infrastructure is generally built for a temperate maritime climate and is not designed around the kind of sustained, severe cold and heavy snow that is routine in more continental climates. That mismatch — well-understood by UK meteorologists but still disruptive in practice — meant that even a relatively brief severe cold snap could paralyze roads, railways, and schools on a scale that would be considered a minor inconvenience in a country built for winter weather.

The Role of the Polar Vortex

The Beast from the East became one of the most widely cited UK examples of how a sudden stratospheric warming event, disrupting the polar vortex thousands of kilometres away over the Arctic, can translate into severe, disruptive cold weather across Western Europe weeks later. It remains a standard reference case in UK meteorological education for explaining this connection to the public.

Aftermath

The scale of transport disruption prompted reviews by rail operators and highways authorities into cold-weather resilience planning, including salt and grit stockpiling and driver-stranding protocols. The Beast from the East remains the reference point for severe cold and snow disruption in the UK, discussed alongside the winter of 1962-63 as one of the two defining UK cold-weather events of the past 60 years.

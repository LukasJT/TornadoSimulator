The Edwardsville, Illinois Amazon Warehouse Tornado of December 10, 2021

On the night of December 10, 2021, an EF3 tornado struck an Amazon.com fulfillment center in Edwardsville, Illinois, near St. Louis. A large section of the warehouse's reinforced-concrete-walled structure partially collapsed under the tornado's winds, killing six workers inside and turning the building into one of the defining images of that night's historic outbreak.

What Happened

The Edwardsville tornado formed as part of the same extraordinary severe weather system that, hours later and roughly 150 miles southeast, produced the long-track EF4 quad-state tornado that devastated Mayfield, Kentucky. Across the evening of December 10 into the early hours of December 11, the system produced dozens of tornadoes across the Midwest and Mid-South — an almost unheard-of outbreak for December.

In Edwardsville, the tornado tracked directly into the Amazon delivery station, a large single-story warehouse building. The tornado's winds caused a substantial portion of the structure's walls and roof to collapse inward, trapping workers under debris.

The Workers Inside

The warehouse was operating with a workforce swollen by seasonal holiday hiring, including many temporary and newer employees who were less familiar with the building's severe-weather procedures than longer-tenured staff. Workers had reportedly been directed to a designated shelter area within the building as warnings were issued, but the section of the structure that collapsed proved insufficient to protect everyone against a tornado of that intensity.

  • Six workers killed when a section of the warehouse collapsed
  • The building was staffed above its typical level for the holiday shipping season
  • Rescue crews worked through the night and following day searching the debris field
  • The collapse became one of the most scrutinized workplace disasters tied to a US tornado in decades

Investigation and Fallout

The US Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) opened an investigation into the collapse, examining the building's construction, Amazon's severe-weather emergency procedures, and whether the shelter-in-place plan and communication to workers were adequate for the danger. The disaster prompted a broader national conversation — well beyond Amazon specifically — about how large warehouses, distribution centers, and other big-box commercial buildings should be designed and operated to protect workers during tornado warnings, including questions about phone use policies, shelter locations relative to exterior walls, and how quickly workers can be moved to safety once a warning is issued.

The Same Historic Outbreak as Mayfield

Edwardsville is often discussed alongside Mayfield because the two disasters happened on the same night from the same weather system, but they represent very different failure points. Mayfield's tornado was a rare, extraordinarily long-track EF4 that devastated an entire small city, including a candle factory where workers were also killed. Edwardsville's tornado was a shorter-track EF3 whose deadliest impact was concentrated in a single large commercial building — making it a more direct case study in workplace severe-weather preparedness specifically.

Aftermath

In the years following the collapse, the disaster has been cited repeatedly in discussions of warehouse and logistics-industry severe weather policy, building-code standards for large single-story commercial structures in tornado-prone regions, and the particular vulnerability of holiday-season temporary workforces who may be less familiar with a facility's emergency procedures than full-time staff.

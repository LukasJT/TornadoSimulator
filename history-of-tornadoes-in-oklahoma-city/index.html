A History of Tornadoes in Oklahoma City

How Oklahoma City's geography shapes tornado risk, with important storms from the Oklahoma City historical record.

Scope: This article covers Oklahoma City and its surrounding metropolitan area. Tornado tracks often cross city, county, state or provincial boundaries.

How geography shapes the risk

Oklahoma City occupies the open central Oklahoma Plains. That setting does not create tornadoes by itself. It influences where boundaries form, how storms approach and which parts of the metropolitan area are exposed as a circulation crosses from one jurisdiction to another.

The recurring weather setup is straightforward: dryline storms routinely encounter deep Gulf moisture and powerful vertical wind shear. Tornado risk depends on the ingredients present on a particular day, so a city’s past is context rather than a forecast.

Core season

April through June, with significant fall events also possible.

Area covered

This guide uses “Oklahoma City” for the city and surrounding commuting region; event paths do not stop at municipal lines.

Important tornadoes in the Oklahoma City region

1999

Bridge Creek–Moore F5

The May 3 violent tornado crossed the southern metro and became a landmark event in mobile Doppler radar and warning history.

2013

Moore EF5

On May 20 a violent tornado crossed Moore, killing 24 people and heavily damaging schools and neighborhoods.

2019

El Reno-area tornadoes

A volatile May sequence west of the city underscored how quickly storm modes and paths can change.

What the record can and cannot tell us

Older tornado records favor destructive storms that crossed settled areas. Short-lived rural tornadoes were easier to miss before dense radar, trained spotter networks and widespread cameras. Modern databases are more complete, but ratings still describe surveyed damage rather than a direct measurement of peak wind at every point.

For local research, search the official event database by county or province and date, then read the responsible forecast office’s storm survey. This avoids a common mistake: assigning every tornado reported near Oklahoma City to the city proper.

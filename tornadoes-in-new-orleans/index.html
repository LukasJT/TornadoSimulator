Tornadoes in New Orleans

A chronological guide to tornadoes in New Orleans, including landmark events, local patterns and official research sources.

Scope: This article covers New Orleans and its surrounding metropolitan area. Tornado tracks often cross city, county, state or provincial boundaries.

A city and metro tornado chronology

New Orleans has experienced tornadoes through several eras of forecasting, construction and urban growth. The entries below are landmarks rather than a complete list. They show how the regional threat has appeared in the historical record and why a metro-wide view is more useful than a city-limits-only count.

1983

Kenner tornado

A strong tornado struck the western metropolitan area, damaging homes and businesses near New Orleans.

2017

New Orleans East EF3

A February 7 tornado cut through neighborhoods in New Orleans East and became the city’s first EF3-rated tornado.

2022

Arabi EF3

A March tornado crossed the Mississippi River into St. Bernard Parish, causing severe damage in the inner metro.

The pattern behind the dates

New Orleans sits in the Mississippi delta’s flat, water-fragmented coastal plain. Here, Gulf moisture is abundant and rotation can develop in fronts, squall lines and tropical cyclone rain bands. The main window is late winter through spring and during hurricane season, but the event list shows why residents should follow current forecasts instead of treating tornado season as a fixed calendar.

Urban growth changes exposure. A path that once crossed fields may now cross schools, warehouses, apartment buildings and major roads. Population growth does not necessarily mean tornadoes are becoming more frequent at one address; it means a similar path can affect more people and infrastructure.

How to investigate another New Orleans tornado

Start with the date and location in an official storm-event archive. Then check the local forecast office’s damage survey for the path, rating and evidence. Contemporary reporting can explain the community impact, but the survey is the better source for meteorological details.

Historical totals should be read carefully. Reporting practices, radar coverage and damage-survey methods have changed. The Fujita scale used before 2007 and the Enhanced Fujita scale used afterward are related damage scales, but their labels should not be treated as instrument readings.

The 2010-11 Queensland Floods

Across December 2010 and January 2011, an exceptionally wet La Niña season caused catastrophic flooding across roughly three-quarters of the Australian state of Queensland. The disaster culminated on January 10, 2011, when a sudden, extremely violent flash flood tore through Toowoomba and down into the Lockyer Valley, an event so ferocious it became known as the "inland tsunami."

What Happened

Weeks of unusually heavy rain across Queensland, driven by one of the strongest La Niña patterns in decades, left river catchments across the state saturated and swollen through December 2010. On January 10, 2011, a localized but extraordinarily intense burst of rainfall over the Toowoomba region — a city built on top of the Great Dividing Range, nowhere near the coast — sent a wall of water surging through the city's streets and then cascading down into the Lockyer Valley below.

The flood struck the small town of Grantham in the Lockyer Valley with such speed and force that cars and houses were swept away with almost no warning, giving rise to the "inland tsunami" description that dominated Australian media coverage in the days that followed.

Damage and Impact

More than 35 people were killed across the full flooding event, with a significant share of the deaths concentrated in the Toowoomba and Lockyer Valley flash flood alone. Days later, the same rain system's floodwaters moved down the Brisbane River system and inundated large parts of Brisbane, Australia's third-largest city, flooding thousands of homes and businesses in its worst flood in decades.

  • More than 35 fatalities across the full event
  • Roughly three-quarters of Queensland declared a disaster zone
  • The town of Grantham devastated by the January 10 flash flood
  • Thousands of homes and businesses flooded in Brisbane days later

Why Toowoomba Flooded So Violently

Toowoomba's flash flood defied the popular assumption that only low-lying coastal areas flood catastrophically. Extremely intense, localized rainfall over the city's elevated position on the Great Dividing Range generated enormous runoff that had nowhere to go but downhill, gathering speed and force as it funnelled into the valley below — a stark illustration that flash flood risk depends on rainfall intensity and terrain, not proximity to the coast.

A Defining Australian Flood Disaster

The 2010-11 Queensland floods remain one of the costliest natural disasters in Australian history and led to a major state inquiry into flood warning systems, dam management on the Brisbane River's Wivenhoe Dam, and land-use planning in flood-prone areas. The Grantham disaster in particular reshaped Australian thinking about flash flood risk in inland towns far from any coastline.

Aftermath

Grantham was partially relocated to higher ground in the years following the flood, an unusual and significant response for an Australian town. Brisbane and Toowoomba both invested heavily in improved flood warning and river management systems, and the January 2011 disaster remains the reference event whenever Queensland's flood risk during La Niña seasons is discussed.

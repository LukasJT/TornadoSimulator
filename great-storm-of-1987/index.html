The Great Storm of 1987

On the night of October 15-16, 1987, an exceptionally deep area of low pressure raced across the Bay of Biscay and slammed into southern England and northern France, bringing sustained winds and gusts well over 100 mph to some areas. It killed 18 people in England (with additional deaths across France), felled an estimated 15 million trees, and became one of the most referenced storms in modern British weather history.

What Happened

The storm deepened with unusual speed as it crossed the Bay of Biscay, a process meteorologists call "explosive cyclogenesis" or, informally, a "weather bomb." It made landfall on the south coast of England in the early hours of October 16, tracking across Devon, Cornwall, and up through southeast England, East Anglia, and into the near continent. Peak gusts exceeded 100 mph in exposed coastal locations, with widespread gusts of 70-90 mph across a broad swath of southern England.

Although it is popularly remembered as "the great hurricane," it was not a tropical hurricane at all — it was an extratropical windstorm, a type of powerful mid-latitude cyclone that develops from temperature contrasts in the atmosphere rather than warm ocean water. Its intensity, however, rivaled that of a hurricane in terms of structural damage.

The Michael Fish Forecast

The storm is inseparable in British cultural memory from a BBC weather forecast broadcast hours before it struck, in which forecaster Michael Fish told viewers not to worry, because there was no hurricane coming. The remark is often presented as a forecasting failure, though Fish was actually responding to a specific viewer's question about a tropical hurricane possibly approaching — not the extratropical storm that was, in fact, already approaching the UK. Regardless of the context, the clip became one of the most replayed moments in British broadcasting history and remains a byword for a forecast that badly undersold what was coming.

Damage and Impact

The storm's winds toppled an estimated 15 million trees across southern England in a single night — including famous losses at Kew Gardens and the historic avenue at Sevenoaks, Kent, which lost six of the seven ancient oaks that gave the town its name. Roofs were torn off buildings, a cross-Channel ferry was beached at Folkestone, and widespread power outages left hundreds of thousands of homes without electricity, in some rural areas for over two weeks.

  • 18 fatalities recorded in England, with further deaths in France
  • An estimated 15 million trees felled across southern England
  • Widespread structural damage and prolonged power outages
  • Insurance losses that made it, at the time, one of the costliest storms in UK history

Why It Still Matters

The Great Storm of 1987 became a landmark case study in UK forecasting and public communication. In the years that followed, the Met Office invested heavily in improved storm-surge and windstorm prediction, and the episode is frequently cited when explaining why the UK later introduced a national severe weather warning naming system, used for storms such as Desmond, Ciara, and Eunice in more recent decades.

Aftermath

Clean-up from the storm took months in the hardest-hit areas, and some of the felled woodland — including parts of the ancient landscaping at Kew — took decades to recover. The storm remains a reference point whenever a major windstorm approaches the UK, and October 1987 is still cited by British meteorologists as one of the two or three most significant windstorms of the 20th century to hit the country.

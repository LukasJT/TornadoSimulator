The TORRO Scale — How Britain Rates Tornadoes

Why a British tornado is rated T4 rather than EF1 — and what the difference actually means.

If you read a British news report about a tornado, you will not see an EF rating. You will see something like T4. That is the TORRO scale, maintained by the UK's Tornado and Storm Research Organisation, and it works on a genuinely different basis from the Enhanced Fujita scale used in the United States.

Wind Speed, Not Damage

This is the core difference. The Enhanced Fujita scale is a damage scale: surveyors examine what was destroyed, match it against a catalogue of damage indicators, and infer the wind speed that must have caused it. The TORRO scale is defined directly by wind speed, with damage used as evidence for estimating that speed rather than as the definition itself.

That distinction matters more than it might sound. EF damage indicators are calibrated against North American construction — timber-frame houses, US commercial building practice, American mobile homes. British housing stock is overwhelmingly brick and masonry, which fails at different wind speeds and in different ways. Applying EF indicators directly to a British street would systematically misestimate intensity.

The Scale Itself

The TORRO scale runs from T0 to T11, giving it roughly twice the resolution of the Fujita scale across the same range of intensities. Approximate correspondence:

  • T0-T1 — light to mild damage; approximately F0/EF0
  • T2-T3 — moderate to strong damage; approximately F1/EF1
  • T4-T5 — severe to intense damage; approximately F2/EF2
  • T6-T7 — moderately devastating to strongly devastating; approximately F3/EF3
  • T8-T9 — severely to intensely devastating; approximately F4/EF4
  • T10-T11 — super to super-intense; approximately F5/EF5

The rule of thumb is that two T-points equal one F-point, which is why a British T4 tornado is not four times weaker than an American EF4 — it is roughly an EF2.

Where UK Tornadoes Actually Sit

The overwhelming majority of British tornadoes are T0-T3 — weak, short-lived, and often causing little more than lifted roof tiles and downed fences. The UK records a high number of tornadoes for its land area, but almost none reach the intensities routine in the US Great Plains.

The strongest well-documented modern British tornadoes sit around T4-T5: the Birmingham tornado of 2005 and the London tornado of 2006 are the standard reference cases, both causing extensive property damage without fatalities.

TORRO Also Rates Hail

Less widely known is TORRO's companion H scale for hail, running from H0 upward and based on hailstone size and the damage it causes — from H0 (no damage) through to the highest categories describing destructive giant hail. It serves a similar purpose to the T scale: a European-calibrated alternative to scales built around North American conditions.

Why This Matters When Reading Reports

If you encounter a British tornado report and mentally translate "T4" as equivalent to "EF4," you will overestimate the event by roughly two Fujita categories. The practical takeaway: UK tornadoes are real, more common than most people assume, and occasionally genuinely damaging — but the strongest British tornado on record would be an unremarkable spring afternoon in Oklahoma.
